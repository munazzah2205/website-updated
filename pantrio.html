A high-clarity interface designed to reduce the mental load of household management, keeping your digital pantry as fresh as the produce it tracks.

The Problem

Most households are losing track of what they own, wasting food they forgot they had, and buying things already sitting in the cabinet. The kitchen has a management problem — and no one has solved it simply enough.

Food Waste

Forgotten items expiring at the back of the cabinet.

Duplicate Purchases

Buying items already in stock due to lack of visibility.

Mental Load

The exhausting task of manually tracking inventory and creating lists.

Personal story - This started as a personal frustration. I found myself repeatedly losing track of items at home, buying things I already had and missing expiry dates altogether. What seemed like small oversights added up to significant waste and unnecessary expense.

Key Insights:

Manual Entry Barriers

Manual Entry is a Barrier to Habit, 3 of 5 participants requested barcode scanning unprompted. Manual entry is the number one reason grocery apps get abandoned.

Spatial Organization

Spatial Organization is the Core Mental Model. Every participant described their kitchen by location — fridge, cabinet, pantry. The app must mirror that, not override it.

Expiry Tracking

Expiry is the Primary Pain Point. Food expiring unnoticed came up in every single interview. It is the most tangible daily cost of a disorganised kitchen.

Duplicates - The Invisible Waste

Buying Duplicates is a Silent, Recurring Problem. Participants regularly bought items already in stock simply because they couldn't see what they had. Small habit, big monthly waste.

Competitive Analysis

I analyzed three popular pantry management apps to identify gaps in the market and opportunities for differentiation.

App Strengths Weaknesses

Your Food Inventory

Basic inventory tracking, simple interface.

  • • No barcode scanning, entirely manual entry.
  • • No storage sections by cabinet or location.
  • • No expiry notifications.
  • • Too basic for real household use.

Inventory & Shopping List App

Barcode scanning available, voice search, shopping list feature, export to Excel.

  • • Barcode scan does not auto-populate product details.
  • • Everything beyond product name requires manual entry.
  • • No recipes or nutritional information.
  • • No expiry notifications or pre-loaded templates.

Kitchen Pal

Pre-loaded product cards, barcode scan with product name and photo, tips and nutritional info for pre-loaded items, recipes, planner, shopping list.

  • • Product details beyond name must still be entered manually.
  • • Storage sections are not customizable.
  • • Nutritional info only exists for pre-loaded products.
  • • No health-condition-based suggestions or achievement tracking.

The Gap:

Existing apps require too much manual effort — scanning rarely auto-fills details, storage organization doesn't mirror a real kitchen, and recipe suggestions are disconnected from what's actually expiring. There is a clear opportunity for a solution that prioritizes frictionless scanning, visual spatial organization, and intelligent recipe suggestions without overwhelming the user.

Defining the Solution

Based on research, I established core design principles to address user needs directly.

Spatial Organization

Instead of one long list, items are grouped by physical location (Fridge, Pantry, Main Cabinet) to mirror the user's mental model.

Frictionless Scanning

A robust barcode scanner flow to minimize manual data entry during the 'Add Item' process.

Proactive Expiry System

Visual cues (color-coding) and smart notifications for items nearing expiration to reduce food waste.

Contextual AI Recipes

Integrating recipe suggestions directly on the item detail page, focusing on utilizing ingredients that need to be used soon.

Information Architecture & User Flows

The architecture is built on a three-level hierarchy: Dashboard (Overview) -> Storage Units (Location) -> Item Details. This ensures users can quickly scan high-level stats or dive deep into specific items.

Core User Flows:

Home Screen

Expiry alerts → Quick item access → Storage categories → Recipe suggestions.

Add Item via Scan

Open Scanner → Scan Barcode → Confirm Details (Quantity, Expiry, Storage) → Duplicate Check → Add to Pantry.

Duplicate Detection

During Add Flow → System checks existing inventory → If match found, alert user → User can update quantity or dismiss.

Profile & Settings

Set Dietary Preferences → Add Health Restrictions → Configure Notifications → Track Achievements.

Designing with Google Stitch

This project explored an AI-assisted design workflow, using Google Stitch to accelerate the prototyping process. Detailed prompts describing layouts, components, and data states were used to generate base structures, which were then refined and polished into the final designs.

01 / Define

System Framework

Established a detailed PRD and design system before any screen generation began, ensuring the AI worked within consistent parameters.

02 / Generate

Base Iteration

Prompted Stitch for base screen structures with specific component and layout requirements — for example: "Create a dashboard with an expiring items strip and category filters."

03 / Review

Audit & Align

Evaluated generated HTML for accessibility, responsiveness, and alignment with the design system.

04 / Refine

Editorial Polish

Iterated on specific components manually — adjusting typography, hover states, whitespace, and contrast to meet the established visual standard.

Raw vs Final

Stitch proved highly effective at rapid prototyping and generating complex Tailwind utility structures quickly. However, the final polish — consistent typography, refined interactive states, and balanced whitespace — required deliberate manual intervention and a strong working knowledge of the design system.

60%

Time Saved

Building layouts with Stitch took roughly half the time compared to designing from scratch.

80%

Creative Control

The final 20% of manual effort — refining typography, spacing, and interactions — contributed to 80% of the perceived visual quality.

AI-Powered Recipe Integration

Why AI?

Instead of a static recipe database, an AI integration allows for dynamic, context-aware suggestions. It can prioritize expiring ingredients and adapt to the user's specific inventory, offering realistic meal options rather than aspirational ones.

How It Works?

The AI engine analyzes the user's 'Expiring Soon' list and crosses it with staple pantry items along with saved dietary preferences and restrictions. It then surfaces 1–2 practical recipe suggestions on the Dashboard, reducing the cognitive load of meal planning.

Key Design Decisions

1. Visual Hierarchy over Lists

Utilized cards and iconography instead of dense text lists to make scanning inventory faster and more pleasant.

2. Color-Coded Expiry System

Implemented a traffic-light color system (Green/Amber/Red) across the app to instantly communicate item freshness status without reading dates.

3. Prominent Duplicate Warnings

Designed high-contrast, inline warnings during the add flow to actively prevent duplicate purchases before they happen.

4. Simplified Grocery Lists

Added a simple grocery list feature for creating, organizing, and tracking shopping items through checklists.

5. Friendly, Organic UI

Chose soft greens, rounded corners, and clear typography to make a utilitarian task feel approachable and less stressful.

Reflection

The research made every decision easier. When you know exactly what frustrated five real people you stop second-guessing what to build.

The challenge wasn't just creating a database, but designing an interface that users would actually want to maintain. The AI-assisted workflow with Google Stitch proved to be a powerful accelerator.

It allowed me to rapidly iterate on structural layouts and focus my energy on high-level UX decisions and visual polish. However, it also emphasized the enduring need for human oversight to ensure accessibility, consistency, and a truly empathetic user experience.
